# Visualizer (planned for v0.2)

**Status: planned on 2026-10-03, at the owner's request. Nothing is built yet.** It starts with the owner's decisions at the bottom of this file.

## What it is

Visuals on the Local Visualizer page that move with the song that's playing. The real sound is measured about 60 times a second, and it drives particles, light, colour and the camera. There are several visuals (Visualizer 1, 2, …), each made from a reference picture the owner sends, and more come as more pictures arrive.

The idea comes from [Vizibeat](https://vizibeat.com/demos/iron-maw/), a paid plugin for Unreal Engine (a game engine) that can't be used here. Its method is simple, though, and is what this plan copies: pick one part of the sound (the kick, say), clean it into a smooth signal, and send that signal to everything at once. Nothing is keyframed or tied to one song, so every song gets its own show.

## Separate or together (the owner's question)

**Built in this repo, as a separate Swift package, `visualizer/`, beside `engine/` and `app/`.** It can be lifted out into its own project at any time.

```
visualizer/                  NEW, a package of its own
  Package.swift
  Sources/Visualizer/        hearing the music, the stage, the visuals, presets
  Sources/VisualizerLab/     a small test app: open a song file, pick a visual, sliders, frame time
  Tests/VisualizerTests/     the analyser, tried on generated tones and click tracks; every shader compiles
app/                         Music Organizer: depends on ../visualizer, never the other way round
engine/                      untouched: the visualizer needs nothing from the engine
```

Its rules:

- It uses only Apple's own frameworks (AVFoundation, Accelerate, Metal, MetalKit, Core Audio, SwiftUI). No new dependencies.
- It never imports `MusicOrganizerKit` and never talks to the engine.
- It writes no files. The app it sits in keeps the presets, in its own settings.
- The music app hands it three things: the player item to listen to, the cover picture, and whether the page is showing.

Why not a new project of its own: the hardest and riskiest part is hearing the sound of the app's own player. A separate project would need a second player, and then the hook-up done again later. It would also mean two repos to keep in step, with two sets of secret checks and CI. The Lab app gives the good parts of a separate project instead: visuals can be tried quickly, with no engine, library or profile.

**Lifting it out later:** `git subtree split --prefix=visualizer -b visualizer-only` makes a branch holding only the visualizer, with its history, ready to push as a repo of its own. `app/Package.swift` then points at that repo instead of the folder, a one-line change.

## Is it possible? Checked on the iMac, 2026-10-03

The iMac's graphics card is an AMD Radeon Pro 570X. Its 5K screen is drawn at 2560×1440. A test program drew glowing particles, a swirling ink background and a glow pass, and timed each frame on the graphics card:

| Work per frame | 1920×1080 | 2560×1440 | 5120×2880 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100,000 glowing particles | 2.1 ms | 1.9 ms | 2.8 ms |
| 300,000 glowing particles | 5.3 ms | 5.3 ms | 6.4 ms |
| 1,000,000 glowing particles | 18.2 ms | 17.0 ms | 16.8 ms |
| Swirling ink background | 2.6 ms | 4.6 ms | 18.3 ms |
| Glow (bloom), at half size | 0.4 ms | 0.6 ms | 2.7 ms |

A smooth picture (60 frames a second) allows 16.7 ms per frame; the aim is 8 ms, to leave room for the rest of the app. So:

- **up to about 300,000 particles** per visual. A million is too many.
- **backgrounds are drawn at 2560×1440 at most** and scaled up to the screen. Soft, glowing pictures hide the scaling.
- **glow is cheap**, so every visual can have it.

Two more facts found on the way:

- **`swift build` doesn't compile Metal shader files.** It copies `.metal` files into the build untouched (tested). So shaders are compiled when the visualizer opens, which took 2 ms here. Their code is kept as text inside Swift files, because `scripts/build_app.sh` copies only the program into the `.app`, not resource folders. A test compiles every shader, so a typo fails `swift test`. What CI's Macs offer for Metal is checked in phase 1.
- **Hearing the sound:** Apple's audio tap (`MTAudioProcessingTap`, attached through the player item's `audioMix`) hands over the samples as they play. It should work on library files, YouTube songs and joined YouTube videos, since none of them is an HLS stream (taps don't work on those). **Not proven yet:** this is phase 1's first job.

## The visuals

Numbers are given in the order the pictures arrived. The building order is separate (see the roadmap below). Difficulty runs from ★ (easy) to ★★★★★.

The pictures are references only. Each visual is drawn by code in the same style, and nothing from a picture is copied in. Pictures 1–5 are kept in `~/Developer/MusicOrganizer-research/visualizer-references/`, outside this public repo, because they belong to other people. Picture 6 was only sent in chat, so it still needs saving there as `visualizer-6-starburst`.

### Visualizer 1: Ring & Ink ★★

*Picture 1: a frame of a song's visualizer video on YouTube.*

- **In it:** a logo in a white circle, with a thin white ring around it and a second ring slightly off-centre. Red-and-black ink or smoke, mirrored left and right, with faint stars and dark red edges.
- **With the music:** the ring swells on each kick, and its outline ripples with the spectrum. The second ring lags behind the first. The ink swirls slowly, faster and brighter when the song is loud. The colours come from the song's cover.
- **How close:** very close. The song's own cover goes where the channel's logo was.
- **Needs:** a background shader, the ring, the cover in the middle, colours taken from the cover.

### Visualizer 2: Iron Maw ★★★★★

*Picture 2 and Vizibeat's Iron Maw demo.*

- **In it:** a dark 3D room, a glowing red ring like an eclipse, rings of metal spikes around it, stage lights.
- **With the music:** the camera orbits slowly, rolls and shakes on the kick. Spikes punch outward on the beat and the hit ripples to their neighbours. Lights fire in turn, one step per beat, and the inner ring twists.
- **How close:** a stylised version. The demo uses hand-made 3D models and Unreal's lighting, while ours is built from simple shapes with glow. The movement can match; the richness of the look can't.
- **Needs:** 3D shapes with lighting, a beat counter (one step per beat), camera behaviours.

### Visualizer 3: Particle Wave ★★

*Picture 3.*

- **In it:** a glowing orange-pink line across the middle. Tens of thousands of blue, violet and pink sparks form peaks above it, with a dimmer reflection below.
- **With the music:** the peaks are the spectrum, bass on the left and highs on the right. Sparks ride their peaks with a little drift, then fall and fade. The line brightens and flickers with loudness, and a kick sends a ripple along it.
- **How close:** very close.
- **Needs:** particles on the graphics card, glow, the spectrum. Built first, because it shows straight away whether the sound is being read correctly.

### Visualizer 4: Tendrils ★★★★

*Picture 4.*

- **In it:** a dark round hole in the middle. Thousands of fine, curling strands of blue light, some pink, pour outward, with specks of light along them.
- **With the music:** the strands flow outward and curl like smoke in a slow current. Bass pushes them out faster and swells the hole, highs send sparkles running along them, and the whole field turns slowly. The song's cover can sit in the hole.
- **How close:** close in motion. The picture's hair-fine detail comes from a slow offline render; ours comes near it with trails.
- **Needs:** a flow field ("curl noise") and trails (each frame fades a little instead of being cleared).

### Visualizer 5: Fountain ★

*Picture 5.*

- **In it:** a white-hot point at the bottom, with sparks of every colour spraying up in a widening cone and a soft glow on the floor.
- **With the music:** the spray's height and amount follow loudness, and each kick throws a burst. Highs make the sparks twinkle, and the glow on the floor pulses.
- **How close:** very close.
- **Needs:** a particle emitter.

### Visualizer 6: Starburst ★★★

*Picture 6 (sent in chat; still to be saved, see above).*

- **In it:** lavender-white streams of sparks shoot outward from a centre in 3D, each led by a bright head. Near sparks are big and blurred, far ones small and sharp.
- **With the music:** kicks fire new bursts, and loudness sets their speed and how many streams there are. The camera drifts slowly into the burst, and near sparks go out of focus.
- **How close:** close.
- **Needs:** a 3D camera with depth of field (blur by distance), and bursts.

## Making it immersive

Every visual is built in 3D space, not as a flat picture:

- **Depth:** near particles are larger and softer, far ones small and sharp, with a light fog.
- **A camera that's never still:** a slow drift, a gentle roll and a punch on the drop, made from small behaviours that never show an obvious loop (Vizibeat's method).
- **Glow** on everything bright, as in all six pictures.
- **The whole page or the whole screen**, with the lyrics floating over it or hidden.
- **Calm parts look calm:** each part of the sound is measured against the song's own last few seconds, so a quiet verse and a big drop look different. That works for loud and quiet songs alike.
- **Later:** knowing the song ahead of time (phase 10), so a visual can build up *before* a drop instead of reacting after it.

## How the music drives a visual

Each visual has a few controls. Visualizer 5's, for example, are spray height, burst size and sparkle. Each control is fed through four steps:

1. **Source:** one part of the sound.
   - Sub (20–60 Hz), Kick (60–150 Hz), Low mids (150–500 Hz), Mids (500 Hz–2 kHz), Vocals and snare (2–6 kHz), Air (6–16 kHz).
   - Loudness, for the whole sound.
   - Beat, a pulse on each beat.
   - Spectrum, all 64 bars, for visuals like 3.
2. **Range:** the low and high points that are stretched to 0–1 (Vizibeat's *Normalize*).
3. **Curve:** straight, or steeper so only the strong hits show.
4. **Fade:** how fast it rises and how slowly it falls away (Vizibeat's *Tail*).

Every visual comes with good settings. The controls editor (phase 8) lets the owner change them and save presets.

**For the sessions that build it:**

- The tap copies samples into a lock-free ring buffer. Nothing on the audio thread allocates memory or waits.
- Each frame:
  - a 2,048-sample FFT (Accelerate's vDSP) with a Hann window
  - 64 bars on a log scale
  - the named bands
  - RMS loudness
- **Beats:** spectral flux in the kick band, against a moving threshold. The tempo comes from the onsets' autocorrelation, which gives a beat phase, so visuals can step in time and anticipate the next beat.
- **Auto-gain:** each band is measured against its own last 10 seconds or so.
- **Timing:** the tap sees the sound slightly before the speakers play it, so the signals are delayed by the output device's latency (from Core Audio). A ± setting covers the rest.

## The stage (what draws everything)

- One Metal view (`MTKView`) at 60 frames a second. It stops when the page is hidden or the app is in the background, and idles slowly when nothing is playing.
- Each frame is drawn into a floating-point image, then glow is added and it is toned down to the screen's range (tone mapping). The drawing size is capped at 2560×1440 and scaled to the window.
- Particles live on the graphics card (compute shaders), up to 300,000 per visual. A Quality setting gives Low, Medium or High: 75,000, 150,000 or 300,000.
- A camera with perspective, depth of field and fog.
- A frame-time counter in the Lab and in a debug mode, to keep every visual under 8 ms on the iMac.
- **Flashing limit:** whole-screen flashes at most 3 times a second, the usual safe limit for people sensitive to flashing light. On by default.

**Why this order:** Visualizer 3 comes first because it shows the measured spectrum directly, so any problem hearing the music is obvious. After that it runs easiest to hardest, and each visual adds one new building block: the emitter, then the background shader, 3D depth, trails, and finally 3D shapes. The app gets the visualizer after two visuals, so the owner can use it every day while the rest is built.

**Rough total:** 15–22 sessions, plus the owner's time watching and giving notes.

**Not planned:** splitting a song into drums, bass and vocals (Vizibeat's "split the mix"). It needs an AI model that runs slowly on the Intel iMac, and a large new dependency.

## From a picture to a visualizer (the routine for every new picture)

1. **The owner sends a picture**, or several, in chat, with any wishes ("the line should jump with the kick").
2. **It is saved** as `visualizer-<N>-<name>` in `~/Developer/MusicOrganizer-research/visualizer-references/`, never in this repo.
3. **Claude adds a card** under "The visuals" above: what's in it, how it moves with the music, how close it can get, its difficulty, and what it needs that isn't built yet.
4. **The owner OKs the card**, or changes it, and says where it goes in the building order.
5. **It is built in Visualizer Lab first** and tried on three test songs: a bass-heavy one, a calm acoustic one and a vocal pop one. Claude checks the frame time (under 8 ms on the iMac) and compares saved frames with the picture.
6. **The owner watches it** in the Lab and gives notes, and it's tuned until they're happy.
7. **Its settings become its built-in preset.** It appears in the app as Visualizer N, with a changelog entry and a commit.
